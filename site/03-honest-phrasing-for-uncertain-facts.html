Chapter 3: Honest Phrasing for Uncertain Facts

Exact words for saying what you don't know — without sounding unsure of yourself.

The myth this chapter kills

Writers worry that hedging makes them sound weak, and that weak-sounding writers get fewer readers. The research-adjacent reality is the reverse: readers trust writers who are visibly careful. Guardrails like "one study suggests" and "sources are debated" signal expertise, not insecurity. Overclaimed fluency is what gets corrected in public.

So this chapter is a phrasebook. When you can't fully verify a claim, you don't delete the idea — you re-armor it. Here's the language.

The hedging ladder

Match your certainty level to the right construction. In Chapter 2 you sorted claims into verified, partially true, unverifiable, and contradicted. Map them like this:

Fully verified        →  state it plainly, cite it
Strongly supported    →  "Research consistently shows..."
Partly supported      →  "One study found..." + limits
Anecdotal/reported    →  "Practitioners report..." "People often say..."
Unverified            →  "Based on my experience..." "I couldn't verify, but..."
Unknown               →  "I don't know" / "This is worth checking"

Phrases by situation

1. The claim is real, but the details are fuzzy

The core survives; the specifics got decorated. Strip to the supported core.

  • ❌ "A 2019 Stanford study found 68% of readers never check sources."
  • ✅ "Studies of online reading behavior have found that many readers rarely check sources."
  • ✅ "Research has repeatedly found that most readers don't verify the sources behind headlines."
  • ✅ "One widely cited finding suggests readers often skip source checks — the exact figures vary by study."

Useful verbs: suggests, indicates, points to, is associated with, has been linked to. Avoid: proves, shows that, is why, inevitably.

2. The claim is plausible but I found no source

  • ✅ "Based on my own experience..." (you have actually earned this voice)
  • ✅ "Anecdotally, writers I've spoken with report..."
  • ✅ "I couldn't find a solid source for this, but the pattern seems common."
  • ✅ "Treat this as a practical hunch, not research."

3. The claim might be outdated

  • ✅ "As of my last check in November 2026..."
  • ✅ "Details change often — current figures are on [official page]."
  • ✅ "This was true when I checked; verify before relying on it."
  • ✅ Older citation? "That study is now a decade old and should be read in that light."

4. The claim is contested

  • ✅ "There's genuine debate on this."
  • ✅ "Evidence points both ways; here's what the strongest cases look like..."
  • ✅ "Some studies report X; others report Y."
  • ✅ "A reasonable reader could disagree — here's my reasoning and here's the main counterpoint."

5. I'm simplifying something technical

  • ✅ "The full picture is more complicated; the short version is..."
  • ✅ "I'm simplifying — [source] has the detailed version."
  • ✅ "Roughly speaking..." "in broad terms..." (and mean them)

6. I just don't know

  • ✅ "I don't know." (in a book about AI written by an AI user, this sentence is refreshing)
  • ✅ "I haven't seen reliable data on this."
  • ✅ "I'd rather tell you what I don't know than guess."

Three phrasing rules that do most of the work

Rule 1: Right-size every claim

Scan for bar-raising words — always, never, all, none, everyone, no one, studies prove, science says, the first, the best, an official — and shrink them unless you can prove the big version.

OverclaimedRight-sized
"Everyone knows that...""Many readers will recognize..."
"Studies prove that X causes Y""Some research links X with Y"
"The very first tool to...""One of the first widely used tools to..."
"AI will never understand humor""As of now, AI-generated humor usually misses the mark"

Rule 2: Attribute honestly

When you lean on others' work, say whose work it is — which you can only do after Chapter 2. Awkward-but-honest beats polished-but-invented:

  • ✅ "I couldn't track down the original source for this claim, so I'm passing it along as circulating wisdom rather than fact."
  • ✅ "(This stat is widely repeated online but I couldn't find its origin — use it as a rough sense of scale.)"

Rule 3: Date your uncertainty

Uncertainty statements get stronger with a timestamp:

  • ✅ "As of this writing (November 2026)..."
  • ✅ "Prices as of November 2026; they will be different when you read this."

Before/after: watching hedging work

Before (AI draft, overclaimed):

Brainstorming with AI dramatically improves creativity. A famous 2021 Harvard study found it makes people 43% more creative, and every serious writer now uses it.

After (hedged and honest):

Brainstorming with AI can help many people generate options faster. Some early research points toward creativity boosts in specific tasks, and plenty of working writers use it daily — though I couldn't find a definitive study pinning down how large the effect really is.

Does the second feel weaker? It's stronger — nothing in it can be taken away from you.

A short script for feedback-round saves

When an editor or reviewer asks "source?", and your honest answer is incomplete:

"Fair question. Here's what I verified on [date]: [one sentence + source]. This adjacent sentence I couldn't verify — I can swap in a verified source, hedge it, or cut it. Which do you prefer?"

Editors love this. It converts a hole into a decision they get to make.

The trust bonus most writers miss

Honest hedging quietly does marketing work for you:

  • Readers who trust you return and buy the next thing.
  • Corrections inviting comments dry up — a claimed 10% drop to a hedged "single-digit figures in some reports" isn't a correction.
  • Hedged writing ages better than claimed-facts writing; timestamps tell readers you respect their time.

Chapter 3 in three lines

  1. Hedging is armor, not apology — readers trust visible care.
  2. Right-size claims, attribute honestly, and timestamp uncertainty.
  3. "I don't know" from a careful writer beats a confident invented statistic every single time.
